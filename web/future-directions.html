Ideas to explore next

Things deliberately left out of the current tool, kept here to raise with your instructor. The tool as built is complete and correct for its job; these are optional next steps, not gaps.

1 · A "tail-targeted" design worth discussing

The idea in one line: instead of learning the whole bell curve and then extrapolating out to an extreme level (like 1-in-a-million), concentrate the tests right near that extreme level so you measure that one number as tightly as possible.

Your current tool (Neyer / D-optimal)A tail-targeted design (c-optimal / 3POD)
Spreads tests to learn the whole distribution (average + spread) well.Puts tests out near the one extreme level you care about.
Gets the extreme (e.g. 1-in-a-million) level by extrapolation — with a wider ±.Pins that specific extreme level tightly.
Best overall picture.Worse at the overall average/spread — it trades breadth for one sharp number.

Why it might matter for your drop test: if what your instructor ultimately wants is a single extreme tail number — e.g. the 1-in-a-million level at 95% confidence — a tail-targeted design could deliver it more tightly, possibly with the same or fewer printed parts.

The honest catch: this is a different, bigger method — a new engine, not a small tweak to the current tool. It comes from the idea of c-optimality, and is realised in the 3POD method (Wu & Tian, 2014) and its refinement 3POD2.0 (2020). The commercial SenTest software also offers a c-optimal option. Our validation and concept pages explain why D-optimal (what you have) is deliberately not tail-optimal.

Question for your instructor: is the goal the whole distribution (average, spread, and reasonable tail numbers) — where your current Neyer tool is the right fit — or is it one specific extreme tail measured as tightly as possible, where a 3POD-style design would be worth the extra build?

2 · A confidence-region (ellipse) plot smaller extra

Because the average and the spread are estimated together, their combined uncertainty forms an oval region on a map of "average" vs "spread" (a confidence ellipse). SenTest can draw it. We left it out because your bell-curve chart and the plain low–high ranges already tell the story for a non-technical reader — but it's an easy add if your instructor would like to see the joint picture.

Both are parked, not lost. Say the word and either can be built on top of the current tool.
