A4 — should we correct the "spread runs a bit low" bias?

Neyer's paper notes that when you only run a handful of tests, the tool's estimate of the spread comes out slightly too low — by roughly 3.5 ÷ (number of tests). The question on the table: do we leave that as a documented quirk, or build machinery to correct it?

First, here is what your tool actually does. We ran it many times on a population whose true spread is exactly 1.0, and averaged its answer:

What drives the gap? Your starting guess

Here is the important part. We ran the identical test two ways — once from a wildly wrong starting guess (off by 5×) and once from a sensible one — and compared both to Neyer's line and the true value of 1.0:

GOOD starting guess BAD starting guess (off 5×) Neyer's prediction true 1.00
tests (N)bad guessgood guessNeyer predicts
200.600.910.83
400.860.950.91
600.950.980.94

So the earlier "mismatch" was not a flaw — it was a bad starting guess paying a one-time "search tax". Neyer's number assumes you already start near the right region; give the tool a sensible starting height and it lands right where the paper says. And with very few tests (~10) a bad guess bites hardest — the tool can run out of parts still searching and never reach an answer at all. The fix is the same either way: a reasonable starting height, and plenty of parts.

What this means for the decision

The direction and trend of the bias are correct and demonstrable — you can show an instructor this chart and say "my tool reproduces Neyer's known small-sample bias, and it shrinks as the sample grows, just as the paper says." What is hard is pinning the exact −3.5/N number into an automated pass/fail test: that figure is an idealised value (it assumes every test sits perfectly at the sweet spots), so a real run drifts a bit around it. Forcing it into a tight tolerance would be dishonest.

Note the confidence tests that are automated (the variance formulas) already pass exactly — only the bias coefficient was left out, on purpose, rather than faked.

Recommendation: leave it as-is, documented (Option 1). Keep the exact variance test passing; document the bias as a known finite-sample quirk with this chart as evidence. It is the honest, defensible position — and it is stronger for an instructor than a green light hiding an idealised number. The alternative (Option 2, a full bias study with larger samples and careful handling of the runs that can't be scored) is real work for little gain, since the effect is already shown to behave correctly.
