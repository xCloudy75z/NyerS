The smart-placement method (Neyer, D-optimal)

Barry T. Neyer

Neyer, B. T. (1994). "A D-Optimality-Based Sensitivity Test." Technometrics, 36(1), 61–70.

The problem

The staircase wastes items when your starting guess is off, and isn't good at measuring the spread of breaking points. Can we do better on both at once?

What it added

Instead of fixed steps, it chooses each next test at the level that teaches the most, given everything seen so far. It self-corrects from a wildly wrong start, and it is built to pin down both the average breaking point and the spread. That clever placement step is what first required a computer. (It's the method the rest of this site is about — see the concept guide.)

Its weakness

It's weaker when you specifically want a far-extreme level — the point where, say, 99.9% break — rather than the overall picture. That gap is exactly what the later methods target.

Think of it as: a searcher who, after each "warmer / colder", doesn't just step a fixed distance — they move to wherever the next clue will be most revealing, and they close in even from a bad first guess.
How it connects to your drop test: this is your method. It picks each drop height for you, self-corrects from your rough guess, and returns the average breaking height and the spread. The "all-fire / no-fire" tail numbers are the far-extreme levels where it's a little less certain — which is why the 2014 methods below exist.
