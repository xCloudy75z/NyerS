1 · What the method does

Some things can only be tested by breaking them, and each test tells you only one thing: it broke or it didn't — never how close it came. This method decides where to test next so each costly, one-use test teaches the most, and from the pattern of breaks and survives it works out two numbers: the average breaking point and the spread (how much it varies part to part).

2 · It's a normal distribution (the bell curve)

This is the heart of it, and exactly what "normal distribution" and "standard deviation" mean here. The method assumes your parts' breaking heights follow a normal distribution — a bell curve. The two numbers it reports are the two numbers that define any bell curve:

  • the average is the centre (mean) of the bell, and
  • the spread is the standard deviation — the bell's width.
↑ top

3 · How it works — three stages

1 · Find the zone. Start near a rough guess and reach further out, roughly doubling the step each time, until you've seen both a break and a survive. Works even if the guess is way off — it just costs a few extra items.
2 · Close the gap. Narrow in between the highest survive and the lowest break, until the results overlap (a lower level breaks while a higher one survived). Until that happens, there isn't yet enough information for a real answer.
3 · Refine. Now a real answer exists. Each remaining test goes at the single most-informative level, sharpening the average and the spread. Stop when the items run out and report the answer with its confidence.
↑ top

4 · Why it tests slightly off-centre

The method does not test at its best guess of the average — it tests slightly to the side. You're after two numbers. The dead-centre (even-odds) level is best for pinning the average, but tells you nothing about the spread — you only see how fast "survives" turns into "breaks" by testing out on the slope. So it picks the one spot that's the best deal for learning both at once (about 1.14 standard deviations off centre). That single idea is the whole method — "D-optimal" just means "the most informative overall choice."

6 · Why more parts help

The method gets sharper the more items you spend: every "±" shrinks, and the small-sample quirk in the spread (section 10) fades. With only a handful the answer is rough and can even fail to finish; with a couple of dozen it becomes reliable. Since you can print freely, that's a real advantage — spend parts to buy certainty.

7 · Finding a starting height (a worked example)

You don't need to know where it breaks — the method self-corrects. A rough guess just saves items. A quick ladder finds one:

  1. Drop a spare part from a small height (a few mm). Survived? Go higher.
  2. Roughly double the height each time — 5, 10, 20, 40, 80 mm — a fresh part each time, until one breaks.
  3. You now have a rough bracket: the last height that survived and the first that broke.

Example: survivors stop near 40 mm and the first break is near 60 mm → your starting guess is a low end of 40, a high end of 60, and a rough spread of about 5 mm. The method takes it from there.

8 · Reading the results (a worked example)

The answer comes out as four numbers (example values, in mm):

  Average breaking point : 52.00  +/- 1.80
  Spread (std dev)       :  4.50  +/- 1.40
  All-fire  (99.9% break)  : 65.90  +/- 4.90
  No-fire   (99.9% survive): 38.10  +/- 4.90
  • Average breaking point — the height where about half your parts break.
  • Spread — how much the breaking height varies part to part (your prints' consistency). This is the standard deviation.
  • All-fire — drop from here (or above) and essentially every part breaks.
  • No-fire — stay below this and essentially none break (a safe height).
  • Each +/- is the confidence: smaller = more certain, and more parts shrink it.
↑ top

9 · Why the all-fire / no-fire numbers are fuzzier

The method places its tests near the middle of the bell, because that's where each test teaches the most about the whole curve. But the all-fire and no-fire levels are far out on the tails (~3 standard deviations), where it put few or no tests. To report them it has to stretch the fitted bell far beyond where the data actually sits — and any stretch like that is less certain, so those numbers get a wider +/-.

A study designed to nail one tail would instead put its tests out near that tail — tighter on that one number, but worse at the average and spread. It's a trade-off: this method gives the best overall picture, at the cost of slightly fuzzier edges. So always quote all-fire / no-fire with their +/-, never on their own.

10 · Why "spread" reads a little low on few tests

The spread estimate comes out slightly low on small samples, by about 3.5 ÷ (number of tests). First, run from a wildly wrong starting guess (true spread = 1.0):

What drives the gap below the theory line? The starting guess. The same test, run from a bad guess vs a good one:

So it isn't a flaw — a bad guess pays a one-time "search tax," worst when items are few. The fix is the same either way: a reasonable starting guess, and plenty of items.
