The staircase method (Bruceton / up-and-down)

Dixon & Mood

Dixon, W. J., & Mood, A. M. (1948). "A Method for Obtaining and Analyzing Sensitivity Data." Journal of the American Statistical Association, 43(241), 109–126.

The problem

How do you find a breaking point at all, when every test only says "broke" or "didn't", and destroys the item in the process? This is the founding question of the whole field.

What it did

A simple rule that needs no computer: after each test, step up by a fixed amount if the item survived, down if it broke. You walk up and down the scale like a staircase, hovering around the breaking point.

Its weakness

It leans heavily on you picking a good step size and starting point. Guess wrong and it wastes a lot of items walking in from far away. It mainly pins down the halfway point (the average) and struggles to measure how spread out breaking points are.

Think of it as: feeling for a light switch in the dark by sliding your hand up and down the wall a fixed inch at a time — fine if you start near it, slow if you don't.
How it connects to your drop test: this is the "obvious" way you might have done it by hand — drop, step up or down a fixed few mm, repeat. It works, but with your scarce parts you'd waste many if your first height is off, and you'd learn little about part-to-part variation. That's exactly the gap Neyer (1994) closes.
