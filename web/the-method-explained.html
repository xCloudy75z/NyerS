Finding the breaking point of a 3D-printed part — how the tool works, and why its answers can be trusted

A complete, plain-language explanation of the method behind the tool: what question it answers, how it decides what to test, how to read its results, and the evidence that its numbers are correct — worked through on a real impact drop-test.

1The question we are really askingand why one number isn't enough

We want to know: from what height does a 3D-printed part break when a weight is dropped on it? That sounds like one number, but it isn't — and pretending it is would be misleading. Print the same part twice and drop the weight from the same height, and one may shatter while the other survives. Materials, layer bonding, tiny print flaws — every part is a little different.

So the honest question has three parts:

  • The typical breaking height — the height at which a part is as likely to break as to survive.
  • How much parts vary — do they nearly all break at almost the same height, or are they all over the place?
  • The safe height and the sure-break height — a height low enough that you can be confident essentially every part survives, and a height high enough that essentially every part breaks — each stated with how confident we can honestly be.

And there is a catch that makes this genuinely hard: every test destroys a part, and each test tells you only one bit of information — it broke, or it didn't. You never learn how close a surviving part came to breaking. You have to reconstruct the whole picture from a pile of yes/no results at different heights, using as few parts as possible.

2The two numbers, in plain termsthe average, and the spread

Here's the idea built from scratch. Suppose you tested hundreds of parts and, for each one, put a tally mark on a ruler at the height where it broke. You would not get one clean number — you'd get a scatter of marks. But they wouldn't be spread evenly: they'd bunch up around some central height and thin out on either side, almost vanishing far away. Stack those tally marks into columns and they form a mound — tall in the middle, sloping down to thin tails on both sides. It is the same shape you'd get by tallying people's heights or shoe sizes: most cluster near a typical value, with a few unusually small or large.

That mound — its formal name is a "bell curve" — is the whole answer, and just two numbers describe it:

  • The average breaking height — the middle of the mound, where the marks bunch up most. It's the most common breaking height, and a part dropped from exactly there is a 50/50: as likely to break as to survive.
  • The spread — how stretched-out the mound is; that is, how much parts disagree with one another. Picture two batches. In the first, every part breaks within a few mm of 300 — a tall, narrow mound: very consistent parts. In the second, they scatter anywhere from 200 to 400 — a low, wide mound: parts all over the place. The spread is a single number for that width — roughly, the typical distance a part's breaking height sits from the average.

A handy rule of thumb: about two-thirds of parts break within one spread of the average, and almost all of them (about 99.7%) within three spreads. That is exactly why the "safe" and "sure-break" heights in the next section land about three spreads out on either side.

What "at the peak" means, concretely: a part dropped from the peak height is a 50/50 — half of parts would break, half survive. Move left (lower drops) and the survival chance climbs; move right (higher drops) and the breaking chance climbs. The peak is not "the height parts break at" — it's the height where breaking becomes as likely as not.

3The safe height and the sure-break heightthe numbers you act on

For a real decision you rarely want the 50/50 height — you want the edges. The tool reports two, in plain terms:

  • The safe height (the "no-fire" level) — low enough that about 99.9% of parts survive. Drop from below it and you can be confident the part is fine.
  • The sure-break height (the "all-fire" level) — high enough that about 99.9% of parts break. Above it, breaking is all but guaranteed.

These sit far out in the tails of the curve, and the tool is honest that they are less certain than the average: pinning down a "1-in-1000" edge takes more parts than pinning the middle, and the tool tells you so rather than pretending. (It even refuses to print a safe-height number it can't stand behind, and instead tells you roughly how many more parts you'd need.)

4How the tool decides where to testthe idea that makes it efficient

A naïve approach tests at a fixed ladder of heights. This method does something smarter: after every single drop, it re-reads everything it has seen so far and puts the next part exactly where that one result will teach it the most. Two ideas make it work:

It tests slightly off-centre — on purpose. The dead-centre 50/50 height is the best place to pin the average, but a break-or-survive there tells you almost nothing about the spread. You only learn the spread by testing out on the slope, where the odds are changing. So the tool tests a little to the side — the single spot that teaches it the most about both numbers at once. That one insight is the heart of the method.

It runs in three plain stages, and it corrects itself even if your starting guess is badly wrong:

Stage 1
Find the zone
reach outward, in growing steps, until it has seen both a break and a survive
Stage 2
Close the gap
narrow between the highest survivor and the lowest breaker
Stage 3
Refine
place each remaining part at the single most useful height

Because the final numbers are read from the results themselves — not from your starting guess — a rough guess is perfectly fine. A bad guess just costs a few extra parts while it finds the zone.

5A worked example — your drop teststart to finish, in real terms

Illustrative example — plausible numbers, to show the shape of a run

The setup

You're impact-testing a 3D-printed bracket. You think it probably breaks somewhere around a 300 mm drop, but you're not sure — maybe anywhere from 200 to 400 mm — and you've printed 25 parts to spend. You tell the tool three rough things: the low end of your guess (200 mm), the high end (400 mm), and how much you think parts might vary (say 50 mm). Your rig can't drop from below 0 mm, so you set that floor.

What happens, drop by drop: the tool starts you in the middle of your guess — a 300 mm drop. The bracket survives. It reaches higher — 340, then 420 — until a part finally breaks, so now it has both a survivor and a breaker and knows the zone. It closes in between them, then spends the remaining parts just off-centre, alternating sides, each drop sharpening the picture. You simply drop each part from the height it names and tell it "broke" or "survived." When the 25 parts are gone, it reports:

Average breaking height
315 mm
a 50/50 drop
Spread
42 mm
part-to-part variation
Safe height
185 mm
~99.9% survive below
Sure-break height
445 mm
~99.9% break above
What that tells you, in words: a typical bracket breaks around a 315 mm drop. Because parts vary by about 42 mm, some break as low as ~230 mm and some hold to ~400 mm. If you need to be confident a bracket survives, drop from below ~185 mm; if you need one to definitely break, drop from above ~445 mm. The tool also says how sure it is of the middle — e.g. "95% confident the true average is between about 295 and 335 mm" — and that range would tighten if you tested more parts.

Note on honesty: the 315 / 42 / 185 / 445 numbers above are made up to illustrate the shape of a run — they are not measured results. The one place we quote exact numbers is the validation below, where the tool must reproduce a published result to the digit.

6Why the answers can be trustedthe evidence, four independent ways

The method is not homemade statistics — it is the Neyer (1994) D-optimal sensitivity test, a published and widely used procedure. The concern with any implementation is simple: does it actually compute the right thing? Four independent checks say yes, all landing on the same answer for the same inputs:

  • Every number is sourced. Each constant the method uses is tagged as coming from the paper, from other published work, or as a stated engineering choice — nothing is an unexplained "magic number."
  • The confidence ranges use the likelihood-ratio method — the same approach the commercial SenTest software reports — so a "95% confident between X and Y" statement means exactly what it says: test many times, and the true value falls inside such a range 95% of the time.
  • It is reproducible. Anyone can re-run the self-check and see the same published result appear.

7What it assumes, and where to be carefulstated plainly, because you'll be asked

It assumes the bell-curve shape. Breaking heights are taken to follow the usual symmetric hump (a normal distribution). This is the standard, accepted model for this kind of test; if a material were strongly lopsided, a different shape could be swapped in, but that's deliberately out of scope here.
The extreme edges need more parts. The safe and sure-break heights are "1-in-1000" statements, and honestly nailing a 1-in-1000 edge from a handful of parts isn't possible — the tool says so and can estimate how many parts a target would need, rather than overclaiming.
The spread can read a touch low on very few parts. This is a known, documented property of the method on small samples; it fades as you add parts, and it's written up honestly rather than hidden.

8What the tool is to useso the practicalities are clear

  • It runs in MATLAB or in free Octave, identically, with no paid add-ons.
  • You give it plain inputs (your low/high guess, a rough spread, and how many parts), in whatever unit you work in (mm, cm, m…). You enter each drop's outcome as simply "broke" or "survived."
  • It shows the answer in a results window that explains itself — the headline numbers, the chart above, and a plain-English reading — plus a box to ask "what's the chance of breaking at this exact height?"
  • Before testing, a small planner tells you how many parts to prepare and where to start.
