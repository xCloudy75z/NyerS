Your two starting guesses — a worked example

Before it starts, the tool asks you for two rough hints. Neither has to be right — here is exactly what they mean, using a drop test on 3D-printed parts.

The scene: you drop a weight from some height into a printed part; it either breaks or survives. You think a part probably breaks somewhere around a 50 mm drop — but you're not sure, and identical parts won't all break at the same height.

Guess 1 — roughly where is the average? (avg_low & avg_high)

You don't need the exact average — just a rough range you're fairly sure it falls in. Here: "somewhere between 40 and 60 mm." The tool puts its very first drop in the middle.

Guess 2 — how spread out are the parts? (spread_guess)

Even identical parts don't all break at the same height — one at 45 mm, another at 55 mm. The spread is how wide that scatter is. You give a rough hint of it — here about 5 mm. It is not the average; it's the width of the bell curve of breaking heights.

What the spread guess actually does — it sizes the first steps

Here's the only job of spread_guess: it tells the tool how far to jump while it hunts for the breaking zone. First reach ≈ 2 × spread_guess, then the jump doubles each step until it has seen both a break and a survive. With spread_guess = 5, starting at 50 and everything surviving so far:

In the tool, that's just four lines

% your two guesses + how many parts
avg_low      = 40;   % low end of your average guess (mm)   — was mu_min
avg_high     = 60;   % high end of your average guess (mm)  — was mu_max
spread_guess = 5;    % rough part-to-part variation (mm)    — was sigma_guess
num_parts    = 25;   % how many parts you'll test           — was N
The reassuring part: both are only starting hints. The tool measures the true average and spread from your actual break/survive results and self-corrects — your guesses do not appear in the final answer. A wrong guess costs a few extra tests, nothing more.
