Reading your results

When the test finishes, the tool prints something like this (numbers here are an example, in millimetres, for a drop test):

=== Neyer sensitivity test: RESULT (25 tests) ===
  Average breaking point : 52.00  +/- 1.80 (1 std err)
  Spread (std dev)       :  4.50  +/- 1.40 (1 std err)
  All-fire  (99.9% break)  : 65.90  +/- 4.90
  No-fire   (99.9% survive): 38.10  +/- 4.90
  Note: a D-optimal test is not tuned for the far tails, so these
        carry wider uncertainty than a purpose-built tail study would.

Here is what each line means, placed on a height axis:

LineWhat it means for your parts
Average breaking point The drop height where about half your parts break and half survive. The single best "typical breaking height".
Spread How much the breaking height varies from part to part. Small = your prints are consistent; large = they vary a lot.
All-fire Drop from this height (or above) and essentially every part breaks. Your "reliably breaks" number.
No-fire Stay below this height and essentially no part breaks. Your "safe" number.

Yes — it's a normal distribution (the bell curve)

This is the heart of the method, and exactly what your instructor means. The tool assumes your parts' breaking heights follow a normal distribution — the classic bell curve: most parts break near a central height, fewer break much lower or much higher. The two numbers the tool reports are simply the two numbers that define any bell curve:

  • the average is the centre (the mean) of the bell, and
  • the spread is the standard deviation — the width of the bell.

And the S-shaped "chance of breaking" curve on the what-it-does page is the same bell curve seen another way: it's the running total of the bell as you raise the height — climbing from 0% up to 100%, and passing through exactly 50% at the average. Same normal distribution, two pictures.

What the "+/-" means

Every number comes with a +/- — its confidence. It's how much the number could wobble given how many parts you tested. Smaller +/- = more certain. For example 52.00 +/- 1.80 means "the true average is very likely within about 1.8 mm of 52". The way to shrink every +/- is to test more parts — which you can, since you print your own.

Two honest points to keep in view:
  • The spread reads a little low when you use few parts (see the spread-bias page) — another reason to print plenty.
  • The all-fire / no-fire numbers carry a wider +/- than the average — the method sees the whole picture well, but the far edges are inherently less certain. Quote them with their +/-, never on their own.
The same numbers are also available inside the result value if you want to use them later — e.g. result.mu (average), result.sigma (spread), result.all_fire, result.no_fire.
